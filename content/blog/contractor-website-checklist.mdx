Most contractor websites don't lose jobs because they're ugly. They lose jobs because a homeowner with a leaking roof, on their phone, can't tell in five seconds whether you serve their area, whether you're trustworthy, and how to reach you. Here's the checklist we use when we build or review a home-services site.

## Above the fold (the first screen on a phone)

### 1. Say what you do and where, in one line

"Roof repair and replacement in Austin" beats "Quality you can trust". Homeowners scan for the service and the city. So does Google.

### 2. A tap-to-call phone number, always visible

Put it in the header and make it a real `tel:` link. On phones, a sticky call button at the bottom of the screen is worth testing.

### 3. One clear next step

"Get a free estimate" or "Book a service call". Pick one primary action and repeat it down the page. Three competing buttons dilute all of them.

### 4. Proof, immediately

License number, "insured", years in business, and your Google rating if it's good. Put these near the top, not buried on an About page.

## The rest of the page

### 5. Services, each with a sentence

List what you actually do (repairs, replacements, inspections, emergency calls) with one line each. Better still, give your main services their own pages; that's how you show up for "water heater repair near me" rather than just your brand name.

### 6. Service areas, by name

List the cities and neighborhoods you cover. It reassures visitors and helps local search. A page per major area can help, but only if each one says something genuinely local (jobs done there, typical issues), not the same text with the city swapped.

### 7. Real photos of real jobs

Before-and-after photos from your own jobs beat stock images every time. A photo of your truck and your crew does more for trust than any slogan.

### 8. Reviews that are real

Embed or quote your genuine Google reviews, with names. Don't write your own, and don't use reviews from a different business. Customers can tell, and it breaks platform rules.

### 9. Pricing signals

You don't need a price list, but "Service calls from $89" or "Free roof inspections" answers the question every homeowner has and filters out tire-kickers.

### 10. A short form for people who won't call

Some people will never phone. Give them name, phone, ZIP and "what's going on", and nothing more. Every extra field costs you submissions.

## Behind the scenes

### 11. Speed on a phone

If the site takes more than a few seconds to load on mobile data, people leave. Large images and heavy page builders are the usual culprits. Test it with Google's free PageSpeed Insights.

### 12. An answer when they reach out

A great website that sends leads to a voicemail nobody checks until tomorrow wastes the whole investment. Homeowners with urgent problems call the next contractor on the list. The fix is to [answer every lead within a minute](/blog/home-service-lead-response-automation), even after hours, with an [AI receptionist](/ai-automation-home-services) if you can't do it yourself.

## Quick self-audit

Open your site on your phone, on mobile data, and ask:

- Can I tell what you do and where within 5 seconds?
- Can I call you with one tap from the first screen?
- Do I see proof (license, reviews, real photos) without scrolling far?
- Did it load fast?

Any "no" is costing you calls.
